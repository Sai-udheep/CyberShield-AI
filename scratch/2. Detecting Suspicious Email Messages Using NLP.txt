 A B S T R A C T
Email-based cyber threats continue to evolve, presenting challenges to traditional security measures that 
struggle to keep pace with sophisticated attack methods. This study introduces a machine learning-driven 
framework that integrates Natural Language Processing (NLP) with a Support Vector Classifier (SVC) to 
enhance email threat detection. The proposed model utilizes feature extraction techniques, including one-hot 
encoding, TF-IDF vectorization, and BERT embeddings, to capture linguistic patterns indicative of malicious 
emails. The model was trained and evaluated on a benchmark dataset, achieving an accuracy of 98.65%, 
demonstrating superior performance over conventional spam detection systems. The results show a significant 
reduction in false positives and false negatives, improving email security and reliability. This research 
contributes to the field by presenting an optimized, scalable solution that can adapt to emerging cyber threats 
and enhance automated email filtering mechanisms.

2.1. Natural language processing in phishing detection
The growing threat of the internet and email-based attacks ne-
cessitated the development of more advanced detection technologies. 
Machine learning has emerged as a strong method for analyzing mas-
sive amounts of email data in order to detect phishing attempts. First 
of all, they were used to extract various features from emails, including 
content, URLs, sender information, and headers. These features are used 
as input data for the detection algorithms. ML often used supervised 
learning — being trained on labeled datasets containing examples of 
both innocent and phishing emails [15]. Various classification algo-
rithms are applied, including SVM, Random Forest, Decision Trees, 
Naive Bayes, and more. These algorithms evaluate email features to 
classify messages as phishing or non-phishing. During training, the 
machine learning model learns to recognize patterns and features that 
differentiate phishing emails from legitimate ones. It assigns weights 
or coefficients to features based on the training data. The trained 
model is tested with a separate dataset to evaluate its performance. 
Metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score are used to assess 
the model’s effectiveness. In practice, the trained model is used to 
score incoming emails in real time. If the score exceeds a predefined 
threshold, the email is flagged as suspicious and may be blocked or 
quarantined [16,17].
Machine learning combined with NLP started to be used to clas-
sify emails. These systems used algorithms to analyze email content, 
identify deceptive language, and discern patterns commonly found in 
phishing emails. This shift improved the accuracy of filtering, reducing 
FPs and negatives. NLP advanced to consider the context and semantics 
of email content. Systems began to assess the intent behind the text, 
differentiating between emails containing routine financial transac-
tions and those that raised suspicion. Detecting context and anomalies 
became essential in the battle against sophisticated phishing attacks. 
Moreover, linguistic patterns and sentiment analysis were used to gauge 
the emotional tone of messages. Phishing attempts using persuasive 
or urgent language were flagged as potential threats [18]. In addition 
to analyzing email content, they began to evaluate the effectiveness 
of NLP combined with several other ML models and apparently, NLP 
works best with SVM. The following Fig.  2 shows the popularity of

3. Methodology
Email Spam Classification Dataset available on Kaggle [24] is the 
benchmark dataset utilized in this study. It comprises aCSV file with 
details about 5172 randomly selected email files alongside their cor-
responding labels, facilitating the classification into spam or non-spam 
categories. The CSV file is a structured set of 5172 rows, each uniquely 
representing an individual email. There is also an array of 3002 
columns — the first column anonymizes the email names and instead 
uses numerical designations over recipient-specific labels for privacy 
reasons. The final column in the dataset assigns the binary values of 1 
for spam and 0 for non-spam classification for the subsequent machine 
learning processes. Each cell in the dataset contains the word count for 
the corresponding word in a specific email, the sample of this data is 
shown in Fig.  3.
3.1. Data collection and preprocessing
The foundational step in this study involved data acquisition, lever-
aging the Kaggle Email Spam Classification Dataset as the benchmark 
repository [24]. This dataset has a variety of email messages with 
diverse content for comprehensive training and testing stages. The pre-
processing went through several stages. This started with lowercasing, 
tokenization, and then the elimination of stop words:
- Lowercasing: the first step in standardizing the textual content of 
emails. All letters within the emails were converted to lowercase. 
This way, any discrepancies caused by variations in letter cases were 
eliminated, ensuring a consistent and standard dataset.
- Tokenization: the next step was tokenization. It was done by breaking 
down the email content into individual units, referred to as tokens, to 
do a more detailed analysis of linguistic features.
- Stop Word Removal: uninformative terms such as ‘‘the’’, ‘‘is’’, and 
‘‘and’’, were removed from the tokenized text. This helped to enhance 
the analyses by focusing on content-rich words, reducing noise, and 
refining the dataset for more meaningful feature extraction.
After these steps were performed, the dataset was ready to be fed 
to the NLP processing for feature extraction. .
3.2. Feature extraction
After preprocessing, relevant features were extracted from the pre-
processed email content. This involved using NLP techniques, including 
tokenization, one-hot encoding, and TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse 
Document Frequency) vectorization. Tokenization is primarily used on 
the lowercase and stop word-eliminated email content. Tokenization 
and Stop-Word Removal in preprocessing steps play a crucial role in 
enhancing model performance. As the tokenization, breaks text into 
meaningful units (tokens), enabling models to analyze individual words 
effectively. Besides, the Stop-Word Removal, eliminates common words 
(e.g., ‘‘the’’, ‘‘is’’, ‘‘and’’) that do not contribute significantly to the 
meaning, reducing noise and focusing on relevant content. One-hot 
encoding converts categorical features into a binary format to feed the 
model. Categorical features, such as email subject, were transformed 
into a binary matrix, to ease their incorporation into the feature set 
for subsequent model training. The next step was then to convert 
preprocessed text into numerical features using TF-IDF vectorization. 
This technique assigned weights to words based on their frequency 
in each email according to their occurrence across the entire dataset. 
This weighted representation captures the importance of words in 
distinguishing emails and contributes to making a feature-rich dataset. 
TF-IDF and one-hot encoding also involve a few limitations.
• TF-IDF: This technique quantifies the importance of words within 
a document relative to a corpus. Its strength lies in its simplicity 
and effectiveness for basic text classification [25]. However, it 
fails to capture the contextual relationships between words, which 
can limit its effectiveness in detecting sophisticated phishing 
emails.

To address these limitations, we incorporated BERT (Bidirectional 
Encoder Representations from Transformers) for further and better 
feature extraction. BERT is a state-of-the-art NLP model that gener-

language semantics as shown in Table  1. BERT captures both the left

on subtle linguistic cues. Despite its superior performance, BERT is 
computationally intensive, requiring substantial resources for training 
and inference.
The integration of BERT significantly enhanced model performance, 
particularly in recall and F1-score, critical metrics for phishing de-
tection. Tokenization and stop-word removal further improved model 
efficiency by refining the input data. These procedures ensured that 
the machine learning model received a well-structured and informa-
tive input, enhancing its ability to detect patterns and make accurate 
classifications during the training and evaluation phases.
• Justification for Feature Selection and Dimensionality Reduction 
Trade-offs:
The choice of feature extraction techniques — TF-IDF, one-hot encod-
ing, and BERT embeddings — was guided by their complementary 
strengths in capturing different aspects of email content. TF-IDF pro-
vides a lightweight, frequency-based representation suitable for iden-
tifying salient keywords, while one-hot encoding preserves categorical 
features (e.g., sender metadata). BERT embeddings, though computa-
tionally intensive, offer contextual understanding critical for detecting 
sophisticated phishing attempts. While dimensionality reduction tech-
niques like PCA and LDA were considered to improve efficiency, their 
applicability was limited. PCA assumes linear feature relationships 
and struggles with sparse TF-IDF matrices, often discarding seman-
tically meaningful variance. LDA, though designed for text, requires 
predefined topics and risks losing granularity in phishing-related cues. 
Thus, we prioritized retaining high-dimensional features for classifica-
tion accuracy, mitigating computational costs through optimized SVC 
hyperparameters and parallel processing.
• Comparison of PCA/LDA in NLP-Based Feature Extraction:
PCA is widely used for numerical data but faces challenges with sparse 
text representations, as it may collapse rare but discriminative terms 
(e.g., phishing-specific phrases). LDA, while tailored for topic model-
ing, imposes rigid topic structures that may not align with dynamic 
phishing tactics. For example, LDA could group benign and malicious 
emails under broad topics like ‘‘financial’’, diluting threat signals. In 
contrast, BERT embeddings preserve context-dependent relationships 
(e.g., distinguishing ‘‘verify your account’’ in phishing vs. legitimate 
emails) without requiring manual topic definitions. Thus, we opted 
for direct feature utilization, reserving PCA/LDA for post-hoc analysis 
rather than preprocessing.

3.4.1. SVC structure
The architectural framework of the proposed model is based on 
an SVM classifier, specifically the classifier called SVC that uses the 
features extracted from email messages for distinguishing patterns and 
making classification decisions (normal or suspicious email) effectively. 
The principle of SVC is to separate data points belonging to distinct 
classes using an optimal hyperplane. This hyperplane has a maximum

margin, or distance, to the nearest training data points of any class. Hy-
perplane works as the discriminating boundary between the two classes 
(normal or suspicious email). SVC is good for memory management, 
as its decision function depends on a subset of training points. The 
illustration in Fig.  5 explains how SVC works in a feature space.SVC 
components could be mapped to a more traditional structure as shown 
in Fig.  6.
As shown in Fig.  6, SVC focuses on finding the optimal hyper-
plane for classification rather than learning complex mappings between 
layers. To further explain the above figure, we have:
∙Input Layer (Input Vector): Represents the features extracted from 
the input data. Each feature corresponds to a dimension in the input 
space.
∙Kernel Function: Computes the similarity between data points in 
the input space.
∙Decision Boundary: SVC aims to find the optimal hyperplane that 
separates different classes in the feature space. In the case of a binary 
classification (suspicious or not suspicious), this hyperplane is the 
decision boundary.
∙Support Vectors: data points that are closest to the decision bound-
ary. These points influence the position and orientation of the hyper-
plane.
∙Margin: The distance between the decision boundary and the near-
est data point (support vector) of either class. SVC aims to maximize 
this margin for better generalization to unseen data.
The kernel function, support vectors, and margin are integral parts 
of the SVC’s decision-making process.

3.6. Dataset description and imbalance handling
The Kaggle Email Spam Classification Dataset comprises 5172 emails 
categorized as spam (39.4%) and non-spam (60.6%). The average email 
length is 127 words, with significant variance in content structure 
and vocabulary richness. Feature variance analysis reveals that spam 
emails exhibit higher variance in keyword frequency and abnormal URL 
patterns compared to non-spam emails.
To address data imbalance, we implemented a combination of ad-
vanced techniques beyond standard class-weight adjustments:
∙SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique): SMOTE 
generates synthetic samples for the minority class by interpolating 
between existing minority class instances. This approach helps balance 
the dataset without duplicating data, enhancing model learning from 
underrepresented examples.
∙Under-sampling Methods: We applied random under-sampling to 
reduce the majority class, creating a more balanced dataset while 
minimizing data loss. This technique helped prevent the model from 
becoming biased towards the majority class.
∙Algorithmic Adjustments (Class Weight Modifications): In addition 
to data-level techniques, we adjusted the class_weight parameter in the 
SVC configuration to ‘‘balanced’’. This modification ensures that the 
classifier assigns higher importance to the minority class during the 
training process, mitigating the impact of class imbalance on model 
performance.
3.6.1. Effects on model performance
Implementing SMOTE improved recall from 99.59% to 99.75%, 
significantly reducing false negatives (FNs). Under-sampling slightly 
decreased accuracy to 97.90% but improved precision for spam de-
tection. The inclusion of class weight adjustments in the SVC further 
enhanced model robustness by ensuring balanced sensitivity across 
classes. Combining these methods optimized the balance between recall 
and precision, contributing to overall model reliability in detecting both 
spam and non-spam emails.

4.1. Impact of classification errors and mitigation strategies
FP and FN have significant implications in phishing email detection 
systems. While FPs may cause inconvenience by misclassifying legit-
imate emails as spam, FNs pose a severe security risk as they allow 
phishing emails to bypass filters, potentially leading to data breaches 
and financial loss.
Implications:
∙FP: These can lead to reduced user trust in the filtering system, 
productivity loss due to manually checking quarantined emails, and the 
potential for important communications to be missed.
∙FN: More critical in the context of phishing detection, as these 
missed threats can result in successful phishing attacks, compromising 
sensitive information and causing financial and reputational damage.
Mitigation Strategies:
∙Threshold Adjustment: By fine-tuning the decision threshold of the 
classifier, we can shift the balance between sensitivity and specificity, 
reducing FNs without significantly increasing FPs.
∙Ensemble Methods: Combining multiple models (e.g., SVC with 
Random Forest or Neural Networks) can improve robustness and accu-
racy. Ensemble methods leverage the strengths of different algorithms 
to minimize both FP and FN.
∙Cost-sensitive Learning: Implementing models that assign a higher 
penalty for FNs can bias the classifier towards minimizing these critical 
errors.
These strategies have been tested, with threshold adjustments reduc-
ing FNs by 20% and ensemble methods achieving a balanced reduction 
in both FP and FN, improving the system’s overall reliability.
However, the presence of FPs and FNs in the confusion matrix, even 
if very few compared to true positives and negatives, suggests areas for 
improvement. One possible explanation for FPs could be the inherent 
complexity and the varying nature of email content, which leads to 
misclassifications. Similarly, FNs may occur due to very fine details in 
email text that are challenging for the model to detect accurately.
Despite these limitations, our results aligned with previous studies 
on email classification and outperformed most of them. Theoretical im-
plications included the potential for enhancing email security and filter-
ing mechanisms to mitigate the risk of phishing attacks and information 
breaches.
In light of the previously stated, practical applications of our work 
could include developing more advanced email filtering systems that 
can accurately identify suspicious emails and also minimize FPs and 
FNs. This could have significant advancements for cybersecurity in a 
lot of domains, including business, healthcare, and personal communi-
cation.
5. Conclusion
This study proposed a robust email security framework that lever-
ages machine learning and NLP techniques to improve the detection of 
suspicious email messages. By integrating SVC with advanced feature 
extraction methods, including BERT embeddings, the model achieved 
an accuracy of 98.65%, surpassing many traditional spam detection ap-
proaches. The findings highlight the effectiveness of the proposed sys-
tem in reducing false positives and false negatives, thereby enhancing 
the reliability of email communication. Despite its high performance, 
challenges such as dataset bias and generalizability across different 
languages remain areas for future improvement. Expanding the frame-
work to support multilingual datasets and optimizing feature selection 
strategies will further refine its effectiveness. This research underscores 
the potential of AI-driven email security solutions in mitigating cyber 
threats and safeguarding digital communication channels.
5.1. Limitations and future work
While our study demonstrates high accuracy in phishing email 
detection, several limitations need to be acknowledged:
• Dataset Scope and Real-World Applicability
While the Kaggle Email Spam Classification Dataset provides a stan-
dardized benchmark for evaluating our model, it may not fully capture 
the diversity and evolving tactics of real-world phishing emails, par-
ticularly in organizational settings. The dataset’s preprocessed nature 
and lack of contextual metadata (e.g., sender reputation, organizational 
hierarchies) limit its generalizability to dynamic, enterprise-level email 
threats. This constraint underscores the need for validation on real-
world corporate datasets to assess the model’s robustness in practical 
deployments.
• Overfitting Risks
Despite using cross-validation techniques, there is a potential risk of 
overfitting, especially with complex models like BERT. Regularization 
techniques and more extensive datasets can help mitigate this issue.
• Language and Domain Generalizability
Our model was primarily trained on English-language emails, which 
may limit its effectiveness on emails in other languages or cultur-
ally specific phishing lures. Future work will focus on multilingual 
datasets and domain adaptation techniques to enhance cross-lingual 
and cross-domain generalizability.
• Inherent Dataset Biases
The dataset may contain biases due to its source and labeling meth-
ods (e.g., overrepresentation of certain phishing templates or sender 
demographics), potentially affecting performance in diverse real-world 
scenarios. To address this, future research will involve testing the 
model on external datasets with broader demographic and contextual 
coverage.
